Phishing Fundamentals & Attack Taxonomy

Understand the core definition, high-level lifecycle mechanisms, attacker motivations, and the 6 primary categories of phishing.

What is Phishing?

Phishing is a form of social engineering where cybercriminals deceive targets into taking unsafe actions—such as clicking malicious links, disclosing confidential credentials, opening infected attachments, or wiring funds—by masquerading as trustworthy individuals, organizations, or services.

Unlike malware that exploits software code flaws (such as buffer overflows), phishing exploits human cognitive vulnerabilities: trust, urgency, fear, helpfulness, and curiosity.

Key Takeaway: Phishing does not break technical encryption. It tricks the authorized human keyholder into handing over the keys voluntarily.

Why Attackers Use Phishing

  • Low Cost, High Return: Millions of emails can be dispatched in seconds with negligible infrastructure costs.
  • Bypasses Network Perimeter Defenses: Legitimate email channels and authorized employee logins bypass external firewalls.
  • Credential Theft Over Code Exploits: Stolen valid credentials allow attackers to simply "log in" rather than hack in.
  • Entry Point for Advanced Ransomware: 90%+ of multi-million dollar ransomware attacks originate from a phishing foothold.
ATTACK ANATOMY

How Phishing Attacks Work (The 6-Phase Lifecycle)

01
Reconnaissance

Attackers gather targets from LinkedIn, leaks, and company websites.

02
Weaponization

Cloning legitimate websites, registering lookalike domains, forging headers.

03
Delivery

Transmitting lure via email, SMS, phone call, or QR code.

04
Psychological Trigger

Inducing urgency, fear, or reward to bypass critical verification.

05
Exploitation

Victim submits password/OTP or downloads malicious archive.

06
Monetization

Lateral network movement, data theft, wire fraud, or ransomware.

TAXONOMY

The 6 Core Types of Phishing

Each attack vector possesses unique traits, recognition criteria, and tailored defense countermeasures.

TYPE 1 Mass Spray-and-Pray

1. Standard Email Phishing

Simple Definition: Mass-distributed deceptive emails sent to thousands or millions of recipients at once, impersonating well-known consumer brands like Netflix, PayPal, Microsoft, or banks.
Indicators to Watch:

Generic greetings ("Dear Customer"), artificial account suspension deadlines ("within 24 hours"), mismatched sender email addresses, and deceptive hyperlinks.

Safe Educational Example:
From: "Netflix Billing" <support@netflx-account-billing.com>
Subject: Your subscription is on hold due to declined payment.
Link: https://netflx-update-card.online/renew (Deceptive Domain)
Prevention Best Practices:
  • Never click billing update links inside unexpected emails.
  • Open a clean browser tab and log into your account directly at the verified official domain.
TYPE 2 Targeted Reconnaissance

2. Spear Phishing

Simple Definition: A highly personalized, targeted attack directed at a specific individual, team, or organization, utilizing researched Open Source Intelligence (OSINT) from LinkedIn or social media.
Indicators to Watch:

Contains accurate personal details (your real name, job title, department, current software projects, or colleagues' names) to establish credibility before requesting confidential access.

Safe Educational Example:
From: "HR Benefits Committee" <hr-update@enterprise-corp.co>
Subject: John, please review the Q4 Developer Bonus Allocation spreadsheet.
Attachment: Dev_Bonus_Review_JohnDoe.xlsm (Malicious Macro Payload)
Prevention Best Practices:
  • Scrutinize sender domains closely—look for 1-character typos (e.g. '.co' instead of '.com').
  • Verify unexpected internal documents via internal Slack/Teams before opening.
TYPE 3 Executive / C-Suite Target

3. Whaling (Executive BEC)

Simple Definition: Spear phishing directed specifically at "big fish"—CEOs, CFOs, Board Members, or finance leaders—with the goal of executing high-value wire fraud or stealing sensitive trade secrets.
Indicators to Watch:

Requests for immediate wire transfers, changes to vendor bank accounts, or confidential acquisition details; demands extreme secrecy and urgency to bypass dual accounting authorizations.

Safe Educational Example:
From: "Robert Vance (CEO)" <ceo.robert.vance@private-exec-mail.com>
Subject: CONFIDENTIAL: Urgent Wire Required for Escrow Closing Today.
Message: "I am in transit and cannot take calls. Wire $140,000 immediately."
Prevention Best Practices:
  • Strict dual-authorization accounting controls: No wire transfer authorized by email alone.
  • Mandatory phone callback confirmation using established corporate phone numbers.
TYPE 4 Mobile SMS Attack

4. Smishing (SMS Phishing)

Simple Definition: Phishing executed via Short Message Service (SMS) or mobile messaging apps (WhatsApp, iMessage, Signal) exploiting the higher open and trust rates of mobile phones.
Indicators to Watch:

Short, urgent messages containing shortened links (bit.ly, tinyurl) or strange domains, claiming missed package deliveries, unpaid highway tolls, or bank fraud security freezes.

Safe Educational Example:
Sender: +1 (555) 019-2831 (Unknown Mobile Number)
Text: "USPS Alert: Package #US-8419 held due to $1.95 unpaid customs duty."
Link: https://usps-redeliver-fee.info/pay (Unverified Fake Domain)
Prevention Best Practices:
  • Never tap links in unexpected text messages.
  • Forward suspicious spam texts to 7726 (SPAM) to notify cellular carriers.
TYPE 5 Voice / Phone Pretexting

5. Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Simple Definition: Voice phishing conducted over phone calls or VoIP. Attackers often use caller-ID spoofing and modern Generative AI voice cloning to impersonate IT support technicians, banks, or executives.
Indicators to Watch:

The caller requests you to read back an SMS two-factor authentication code (OTP), claims your computer is transmitting virus signals, or asks you to install remote support software (AnyDesk, TeamViewer).

Safe Educational Example:
Caller ID: +1 (800) 555-0199 (Spoofed Helpdesk Number)
Caller: "Hi John, this is Mike from IT. We detected anomalous malware."
Prompt: "Read back the 6-digit MFA SMS code sent to your phone."
Prevention Best Practices:
  • Never share MFA codes, OTPs, or passwords over the phone with anyone.
  • Hang up and call back the official IT Helpdesk extension from the corporate directory.
TYPE 6 Interception & Cloning

6. Clone Phishing

Simple Definition: An attack where the criminal captures an authentic, previously delivered legitimate email containing an attachment or link, copies it verbatim, and resends it with the legitimate attachment or link swapped for a malicious version.
Indicators to Watch:

A message claiming to be an "updated version" or "resend" of an earlier communication (e.g. "Resending with corrected link" or "Updated contract attached").

Safe Educational Example:
From: "Legal Operations" <contracts@vendor-agreement-portal.co>
Subject: Resending: Revised Vendor Agreement (Previous link expired)
Link: https://contract-signing-portal.co/doc (Cloned Phishing Portal)
Prevention Best Practices:
  • Compare the sender email address against the original email thread.
  • Contact the sender on a separate channel before opening "resubmitted" links or files.
