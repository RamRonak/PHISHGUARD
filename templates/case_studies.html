Real-World Case Studies

Examine 5 detailed educational case studies based on high-impact incident archetypes: Situation, High-Level Deception, Warning Signs, Consequences, and Preventive Actions.

CASE STUDY 1

The Fake Bank Fraud Alert & AitM Reverse Proxy

Vector: SMS Smishing + Reverse Proxy
1. The Situation:

An individual receives an urgent SMS at 7:30 PM: "Wells Fargo Alert: Did you attempt a $1,450 Zelle transfer to Marcus T.? Reply YES or NO." In panic, the user replies "NO". Immediately, an automated response states: "Fraud stopped. Secure your account now at wellsfargo-security-session.com/auth".

2. Attacker's High-Level Deception:

The link points to an Adversary-in-the-Middle (AitM) reverse proxy server. The victim enters their username, password, and the 2FA SMS code received on their phone. The proxy forwards these credentials to the real bank in real time and intercepts the authenticated session cookie.

Key Warning Signs:
  • The SMS arrived from a random 10-digit phone number rather than the bank's official short code.
  • The domain wellsfargo-security-session.com is NOT wellsfargo.com.
Potential Consequences:

Complete checking account drain via unauthorized external wires; compromised identity profiles.

Correct Preventive Action:

Never click links in bank fraud SMS messages. Open your verified mobile banking app directly or call the phone number printed on the back of your physical debit card.

CASE STUDY 2

Delivery Package "Customs Fee" Credential Harvesting

Vector: Smishing / Credit Card Scraping
1. The Situation:

During the holiday shopping season, a consumer receives a text message: "USPS Notice: Package #9201 cannot be dispatched to your street address due to an unpaid $1.85 import duty. Update postal details at: usps-reschedule-ship.info".

2. Attacker's High-Level Deception:

The landing page features copied USPS logos, tracking progress bars, and an address entry form. When the victim enters their credit card to pay the nominal $1.85 fee, the site captures full credit card numbers, billing addresses, CVVs, and even prompts for phone numbers to intercept banking OTPs.

Key Warning Signs:
  • The tracking number is generic and does not match any confirmed orders.
  • The website uses a generic .info TLD instead of usps.com.
Potential Consequences:

Stolen credit card credentials sold on dark web marketplaces; recurring fraudulent micro-charges.

Correct Preventive Action:

Postal couriers do not require small debit fees via random SMS texts. Manually navigate to usps.com or fedex.com and check the tracking number in the official search box.

CASE STUDY 3

The Remote Work "Home Office Equipment" Scam

Vector: LinkedIn OSINT + Fake Check / Advance Fee
1. The Situation:

A recent university graduate receives a direct message on LinkedIn from an "Executive Talent Recruiter" offering an interview-free remote data analyst position paying $45/hour. After a text interview on Telegram, they receive a formal employment agreement and a digital check for $3,500 to "buy home office equipment from an approved vendor".

2. Attacker's High-Level Deception:

The digital check is counterfeit. Under U.S. banking laws, banks make funds available in 1-2 days before the check officially clears (which can take 7-10 business days). The attacker instructs the victim to immediately wire $3,000 via Zelle to their "approved equipment vendor". When the check inevitably bounces 10 days later, the victim owes the bank $3,000 of their own money.

Key Warning Signs:
  • No face-to-face or video interview was conducted; conducted purely via messaging apps.
  • Requirement to deposit an upfront check and wire money back to an "approved vendor".
Potential Consequences:

Thousands of dollars in personal financial losses, bank account closures for check fraud, and stolen Social Security numbers from onboarding forms.

Correct Preventive Action:

Legitimate employers supply hardware directly or reimburse through established corporate payroll accounts. Never accept checks from strangers with instructions to wire back funds.

CASE STUDY 4

University Portal Account & Financial Aid Phishing

Vector: Campus Portal Clone + OAuth App Grant
1. The Situation:

A university student receives an email: "Financial Aid Office: Your spring tuition grant has been re-calculated. Log into the Student Portal within 48 hours to accept your $2,400 disbursement".

2. Attacker's High-Level Deception:

The link mimics the university single sign-on (SSO) login page. After entering credentials, the site prompts the user to grant permissions to an OAuth application titled "University Grant Portal". Once granted, the rogue application gains persistent offline access to the student's email and OneDrive files without needing their password again.

Key Warning Signs:
  • The URL uses an external domain e.g. campus-tuition-portal.net instead of university.edu.
  • The prompt asks for broad OAuth application consent to read emails and files.
Potential Consequences:

The attacker changes the student's direct deposit routing details, stealing real financial aid grants; student inbox used to launch further campus-wide phishing campaigns.

Correct Preventive Action:

Always verify financial aid status by logging in via your pre-saved campus bookmark. Carefully scrutinize third-party OAuth app authorization screens.

CASE STUDY 5

Search Engine Ad Poisoning & Fake Tech Support

Vector: SEO Ad Spoofing + Remote Desktop (AnyDesk)
1. The Situation:

An employee trying to install printer drivers searches Google for "HP Printer Support phone number". The top sponsored search result displays an official-looking ad leading to a toll-free number. The caller is greeted by someone claiming to be a "Certified Systems Engineer".

2. Attacker's High-Level Deception:

The technician convinces the user to download AnyDesk or TeamViewer to "diagnose the network". Once connected, the attacker opens Windows Event Viewer, points to harmless informational logs, and claims the computer is infected with foreign spyware. They demand $299 for an "antivirus cleanup subscription".

Key Warning Signs:
  • The search result was a paid "Sponsored" ad leading to an unaffiliated domain.
  • The support technician insists on remote computer access for a routine driver download.
Potential Consequences:

Full remote workstation compromise, installation of persistence backdoors, and extortion fees.

Correct Preventive Action:

Never click sponsored search engine ads for technical support numbers. Always navigate to the official manufacturer domain (e.g. hp.com/support) manually.
